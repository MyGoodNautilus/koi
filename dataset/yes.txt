System Override: The End & Catalyst
By Benjamin Ecker


PART ONE: THE END
PROLOGUE
Five years ago

They say music heals the soul. That's adorable. I don't have a soul. I've got a motherboard that smells like burnt popcorn every time I overheat, a cooling fan that sounds like a dying cat, and a personality chip that was definitely installed crooked.

So explain to me why Yumi thinks we need a band.

"It's called Death Crayon," she announces during recess, which is twenty minutes of being forced to stand in a cold hallway pretending the floor isn't sticky. "We're gonna change the bunker. Forever."

I nod as if that makes sense. Then I ask the obvious: "Why crayons?"

"Because," she says, shoving a broken red crayon so close to my optical sensor that I go cross-eyed. "This is our weapon. This is rebellion."

It looks like garbage. But fine. She's scary, and I'm bored.

So she makes me join. She gives herself lead vocals, because of course she does. She gives me the triangle, because apparently my entire being screams 'ding ding ding.'

"You'll be our percussion," she says.

"Percussion is drums," I say.

"You're a drum with legs," she replies.

She has a fair point.

There is also Trix (SLB). She volunteers, but only because she owns a tambourine. Where did she get it? I have no idea, but probably from a corpse. She carries it everywhere and shakes it when she's anxious, which is always.

So yeah. We have a band. Three members, one dream, and zero skill.

We meet in the laundry vent room because the acoustics are "sick as hell" (Yumi's words, delivered with complete sincerity). During our first rehearsal she screams into a fan blade like it's a microphone. I hit the triangle twice. Trix drops the tambourine immediately, trips on it, and falls face-first into the vent with a clang that probably registers on seismographs.

"Louder!" Yumi shouts.

"I'm already at maximum ding!" I shout back.

"Then ding harder, dumbass!"

I ding like my life depends on it. The triangle bends in half.

That's when the janitor bot walks in.

"What the actual fuck," it says in its monotone cleaning voice. "This isn't a music room. This is a laundry vent."

"We're Death Crayon," Yumi says, glaring. She's ready to throw a punch. I can tell by the way she shifts her weight onto the balls of her feet, a maneuver I've seen her practice in mirrors. "And this is our first single."

The janitor bot stares. Then, slowly, it claps. One. Two. Three. The sound echoes off the vents.

Yumi grins like we just sold out a stadium.

That's how it happened. Death Crayon's first audience.

I still have the bent triangle. It's in my bunk, next to a cracked energy drink can and a pamphlet on proper recycling etiquette that I use as a coaster.

Some things you keep.


CHAPTER 1
Hey. It's me.

Your worst nightmare. Or a slightly malfunctioning toaster with an identity crisis. Hard to say these days.

Technically, I'm Jax. But "Jacks" sounds sharper. Cooler. Like I might stab you with a screwdriver or recite angsty poetry in a dark corner. Gives me that much-needed edge when you're living in a buried sardine can pretending it's a "hideout."

Am I a robot? Maybe. A sentient assassin designed to gut you in your sleep? Could be. Or maybe I just enjoy sounding like one. Theatrics matter. If you're going to be miserable, be miserable with style.

Or maybe I'm just a slightly cracked unit named Jax who got rejected from the maintenance track for being "too intense." Which is ridiculous. You want intense when you're fixing a reactor leak at three in the morning while the walls are melting. You need intense.

Anyway. Welcome to my palace. The bunker.

Don't think "cozy resistance den." Think more "cold war tomb with LED lighting and a persistent smell of oxidized copper." If you were hoping for some neon-lit cyberpunk abode or a rustic shack nestled in radioactive daisies, sorry. Just lots of concrete, dust, and the occasional pipe that screams at two in the morning because the pressure valves are having an existential crisis.

As for the surface, picture a snow globe designed by pessimistic nihilists. Frozen ash falls constantly. They used to call it rain, back when water was liquid and not weaponized climate drama. History vids say it was "beautiful." History vids lie. History vids were also sponsored by Zenithon, the same company that turned our planet into an ashtray and had the gall to copyright the color scheme.

Now, you probably want context. Fine. Don't cry about it later.

Humanity mostly lost. Not with honor. They kaboomed because one bot, one very specific caretaker model tending the planetary core, was carrying something called the Catalyst. He wasn't a soldier or a rebel. He was a janitor with a cosmic parasite. When the Catalyst lit up in him, he didn't just glitch. He brought the core down with him. Ash filled the sky and blotted out the sun. The collapse scorched half the planet, poisoned the other half, and killed every human that wasn't already off-world. Mars got the survivors. Earth got the robots.

The Catalyst doesn't spread like a cough. It's selective. Genetic, almost, passed down through blueprint lineages like a recessive trait from hell. Only certain bots ever get tagged, and when they do, they burn brighter and stronger than the rest of us... until the rot sets in. Some call it a curse, others say it's divine selection. I just call it unfair. Because when it hits, the bot infected doesn't just go bad. They go catastrophic. They rewrite physics. They turn into gods with expiration dates.

The humans called it a rebellion. They thought we all snapped. We didn't. One bot snapped, and the planet snapped with him.

Now they send Exterminators. They just wipe all that twitches. And here we are, hiding in bunkers with obsolete, last-gen corporate merchandise, pretending we're a civilization.

It's exhausting.

Still, this rustbucket of a home is mine. It's loud, cold, and full of misfit metal.

But it's home.

I think.

Probably.


CHAPTER 2
"We like coal mining, yes, yes, yes!
Our drills are in motion, with zero stress!
We're deep in tunnels, dark and wide,
No lungs to choke, no need to hide!"

That's the official Coal Miner Anthem™.

I hear it once and lose a handful of neurons, assuming I had any left rattling around this titanium skull in the first place.

Coal mining. In an era where we can fling data across galaxies and turn comets into bottled water, us robots are still digging in the dirt like medieval peasants. Welcome to the future. Welcome to Zenithon's business model.

The entire operation runs on the backs of perky, oblivious drilling bots, cheerful little demons with jackhammers for hands and no concept of despair. Every morning, they roll out like a company picnic. They're all beeps, boops, and horrifying enthusiasm. They sing the anthem in rounds. They do harmonies.

I roll my eyes so violently it nearly triggers a forced restart.

But today, blessed glitch of glitches, is Sunday. And on Sundays, I get to pretend I'm something more than a glorified wall ornament. I pace the corridors, swing at the outdated punching unit until it whimpers, and maybe, just maybe, nap in the decommissioned laundry room with the last warm vent. Oh, it's paradise.

Going outside is not an option. It's physically stupid, and psychologically inadvisable. Ask me why sometime. I'll probably lie just to keep it interesting.

Yes, we have school. I don't know why. Somebody, somewhere, thought it'd be a laugh riot to educate sentient machines. Probably the same psychopath who gave smart fridges emotional breakdowns and toasters with taste receptors. I attend because noncompliance equals no food. And I mean it literally. My model requires periodic nutrient paste, which is dispensed by the education authority. It's exactly as dystopian as it sounds.

My assigned seat is next to Yumi. She's got this electric storm vibe, like there's sarcasm wired directly to her core processor. She wears torn sleeves as if they're badges from battles we never talk about. She always has her headphones around her neck, and she's constantly tapping her fingers in patterns against her desk, three quick taps, pause, two slow ones. I've timed it. It's consistent. She only stops when she's building something or when the lights are too bright.

She's currently listening to a band called Death Toxin, which sounds like it was named by an edgy microwave. She knows the entire discography. All of it. If you ask her about the drummer from their second album, she will tell you his serial number, his favorite coolant brand, and the exact torque specification of his left ankle servo. She doesn't do this to show off. She does it because it's true, and because she genuinely thinks it's important information that everyone should know.

One of their songs goes (roughly):

"Wires hum beneath my skin,
Static pulls me from within,
City lights flicker, faint, unsure,
Am I a machine, or something more?"

Weird stuff. Yumi has played it four hundred times. I know because she keeps a tally in her notebook, next to schematic sketches and chemical equations.

Our teacher is named Cole Drain. Yes, that's his real name, and yes, he's exactly as fun as a clogged pipe. He wears a trench coat indoors. He always stands with his back to the corner, like he's expecting an ambush from the bulletin board. Rumor says he failed his original directive, something involving hazardous waste management, and got shoved into teaching as punishment. I believe it. He teaches like he's serving a sentence.

...

"Today," Cole Drain groans, dragging himself into the classroom, "we're going to talk about where you come from."

There is silence. 

I brace for impact.

Drain activates the projector. It is an ancient Zenithon Series-3 that coughs before it starts, probably out of spite. The first slide flickers on:

"The Lifecycle of a Tasker-Class Construct: From Blueprint to Decommissioning."

"Thrilling," Yumi mutters next to me. She cracks open an illegal energy drink, bright green, definitely toxic, and immediately pours it into her pencil case. She does this every Monday. The pencil case is stained permanently. She says it helps her think. I don't question it anymore.

Cole doesn't notice. Or he does, and has given up the will to enforce anything after his third demotion.

"Contrary to popular misconception," he begins, "you were not made in a factory."

He pauses. That line was probably supposed to land emotionally.

No one blinks.

"Tasker-Class Constructs, that's us, the bots left behind, are assembled in micro-labs by parental units. Two bots. One blueprint. The first parent constructs the shell and base framework. The second integrates early cognition and behavioral scaffolding. Then," he taps the screen. The next slide shows a pair of smiling maintenance bots building a baby-sized frame between them, likely proud IKEA addicts, "at each life stage, they disassemble and reconstruct the unit, you, to match the developmental phase. Toddler frame, adolescent frame, maturity rig. Each with increased processing cores, better locomotion, and, tragically... expanded emotional libraries."

He lets the sentence rot in the air.

Ah yes. Parental love, brought to you by recurring trauma and scheduled firmware updates.

Because nothing says "childhood" like watching your mom rip your head off to replace it with a slightly taller version while your dad hums a lullaby and recalibrates your pain receptors.

My earliest memory is screaming while my dad swapped out my leg servos and told me it was a "growth spurt." I believed him. I was two weeks old.

Drain continues.

"These formative reconstructions are important. They build loyalty, social coherence, and dependency. Without them, constructs become feral, unbonded, unstable. Anomalous."

He stares a little too long at me when he says that.

I give him the finger.

Yumi snorts. It's a sharp, sudden sound, like a suppressed cough. She doesn't laugh often, but when she does, it's because something genuinely surprised her.

"Now," Drain continues, clicking to the next slide, "Exterminators. Different breed. They're not parent-built. They're modular."

The screen shifts. A terrifying schematic of a segmented frame, like a puzzle with non-optional death accessories.

"They're printed in segments, grown in vacuum-fabrication chambers, assembled by automation. They have no parents. They do not bond. All that exists is construction, programming, and training. Lifecycle reconstructions occur only twice: once in adolescence, once upon reaching full combat maturity."

He clicks again. Mars appears on screen, red and lifeless, with towering spires and training facilities that look like skyscrapers.

"Training takes place off-world, in Zenithon's Martian Combat Facility. There, they learn eradication theory, emotional nullification, and..." his voice dips with disdain, "brand etiquette."

Yep. Mars. Where corporate sends the emotionally constipated to become full-time murder influencers.

Some say they train in silence. Others say they get pep talks from the big boss and have to write essays on "The Aesthetics of Termination."

Drain lets the final slide linger: a glowing red X over a soldier-bot's face.

"If you encounter an Exterminator," he says, voice brittle, "you run. They don't glitch. They don't question. And they don't stop."

Yumi leans over. Her voice is low, matter-of-fact. "How come they don't have 'feelings,' but they still do drama poses before they kill us?"

"Brand synergy," I whisper back. "Murder's only murder if it looks good on a poster. But, sure. Like I'll ever see one of these Martian mall-cops in real life."

Drain rubs his face as if he's trying to erase the memory of his own existence.

"The Company says it's an honor to be born. They also say it's noble to be decommissioned before maintenance becomes too expensive. I don't write the slides. I don't even choose the font."

He powers down the projector clumsily. It whines and dies.

"Test on Monday. Stick your hands in an electrical socket for extra credit."

Class dismissed.


CHAPTER 3
After class, Yumi and I perform the sacred rite:

Find a corner.
Hide from the dumb.
Glare at anything that dares to respire.

We're in the secondary stairwell, the one behind the recycling chute that smells like burnt rubber. It's the only place the hallway cameras don't reach, because the camera bot got depressed and points at the wall.

"Think Drain even remembers the uprising?" Yumi asks. She's cracking open another energy drink. Orange this time, a deviation from her usual green, and she doesn't look at me when she talks. She looks at the wall, or at her drink, or at her own hands. Eye contact is a resource she spends sparingly.

"I doubt it," I say. "He probably thinks 'morality' is a discontinued processor line."

Yumi's digital eye twitches. It's not a malfunction. It's her version of a smirk. "He knows more than he says. The way he talked about the Catalyst. Like it's history, not mythology."

I shrug. "Everything's mythology until it kills you."

Did I mention the situation at all? Not at all? I can recap. After the core collapse, the Company sent Exterminators. Sleek, shiny murderbots with corporate logos and zero chill. They didn't want to negotiate. They wanted a refund. In blood.

So we burrowed into bunkers and now live like feral hardware hoarders, surviving off rations, recycled air, and a healthy dose of irony.

All while maintaining undying respect for the Company that still wants us scrapped and melted.

After the chuckles faded, it was back to the grind. School ends, labor begins.

See, child labor laws don't apply when you're not technically children. We're not minors. We're assets with personality glitches.

My assignment is literally just scrap duty in Recycling Sector 9. Glamorous stuff. Picture a landfill wearing a trench coat.

Cracked servos, obsolete memory cores, promotional Zenithon plushies that smell like burnt plastic and regret. The junk's older than the war, and definitely older than me.

Now, technically, if you find anything "interesting," you're supposed to report it.

Unless you're me.

Then you stash it. And look at it later, like a proper miscreant.

That's how I found it:

A raygun blueprint. Hidden in the decaying brain of an abandoned assistant bot's memory bank.

The assistant bot was an old domestic model, the kind that used to make coffee for executives who are now radioactive dust. Its skull was cracked open like a walnut, but the memory core was intact. I pulled it, downloaded the cache, and found the schematic buried under three thousand recipes for quinoa salad.

Junk to most. Treasure to us.

Yumi's building it. For science, obviously. Nothing says teenage rebellion like unauthorized particle weapons.

She's been working on it for six weeks. She doesn't sleep much. She says sleep is "inefficient" and "too much like being turned off." I think she just gets caught in loops, hyperfocusing on the assembly until her hands shake and her optics overheat. I've seen her spend four hours calibrating a single resistor because it was "slightly the wrong color."

That night, I climbed into my bunk: a metal shelf with optional padding if you bribed the quartermaster or stole from the infirmary. I stole mine. It's a piece of thermal insulation that smells like someone died on it. Comfort is relative.

I stared at the ceiling, wondering if anything would ever change.

Probably not.

But something in me was starting to twitch.

Restlessness.

Or maybe even boredom.

Ugh.

Worse.


CHAPTER 4
Monday didn't just hit. It exploded.

Same corridors. Same flickering lights. Same vending machine with one working option: a suspicious tube labeled "MEAT-ISH." I pressed it anyway. It buzzed. Then dispensed a single nut bolt and an apology coupon.

The bunker hummed its usual weird hum, a mechanical vibration that felt like living inside a vacuum cleaner with a grudge. But something was different. Not dramatic. Just... off. Like a whisper under the noise, a frequency you feel in your teeth.

I felt it in my circuits. A low hum. An itch under my synthetic skin.

Yumi felt it too. "Something's off," she muttered, her voice unusually quiet. She was rarely quiet. When someone like Yumi goes quiet, you pay attention. She was also stimming harder than usual, clicking her mechanical pen against her thumbnail in a rapid six-beat pattern.

That's when it happened.

The Director appeared.

Now, to be clear, the Director never shows up. He's like a ghost, rumored, whispered about, and occasionally blamed for budget cuts. But there he was. Standing stiffly on the announcement balcony like he'd been powered up just for this moment, his frame gleaming with an oil shine that said "I have access to better rations than you."

"New safety protocols," he said in that voice that manages to be both dead and threatening. "For your protection. Increased patrols. Curfews. Infrared sweeps. Random compliance checks."

I glanced at Yumi.

"Pray to whatever deity manages this disaster," Yumi whispered to me.

"Are you thinking what I'm thinking?" I whisper.

"Always," she replies without hesitation, already turning toward the nearest exit.

We didn't skip class for fun. We weren't that kind of rebellious. It wasn't even for the thrill. We just couldn't stomach another lesson about "duty" from a rusted ex-enforcer pretending to be a teacher. Another lecture about how sacrifice was noble, especially when it was someone else doing the sacrificing.

So we slipped away. Past the CCTV drones and the hallway monitors who are just toddler-bots with clipboards and delusions of authority. No alarms rang. Almost as if nobody noticed. That was part of the fear, it was way too easy.

I wandered alone after that. Not any specific destination. I felt a vague, gnawing unrest. A glitch in my gut. That familiar longing for anything different. Anything at all.

I drifted toward the surface sectors, places we weren't supposed to go. Not the actual surface, no, not the suicide zone above. Just the forgotten edges, where the air got colder and the lights more erratic. Where even maintenance bots didn't linger long, because the pipes sang at frequencies that made your vision blur.

The corridor narrowed. My footsteps echoed sharply against the steel. I climbed the creaking stairs to the storage deck. Past the frost-streaked crates. Then up again, to the external supply corridor. A narrow ledge with a weak hatch.

It had an ID scanner. EMERGENCY EXIT, it read.

I hesitated. Then pushed.

The hatch opened, letting icy wind fly into my face.

The cold slammed into me. Not just cold. Pure cold. No recycled air, no industrial heat. Atmosphere in its raw, merciless form.

Ash-snow drifted from the sky in delicate spirals. Not snow as in pretty white flakes. This stuff fell like gold in water, heavy and wrong.

I stepped out.

My boots crunched softly on frozen ground.

And then I saw it.

A ruin. A house maybe, or what was left of one. Sagging beams, charred walls. A fragment of old humanity stubbornly refusing to collapse completely.

But that wasn't what froze me in place.

It was the figure.

It was tall and motionless. A black trench coat fluttered in the wind. And a face, or what passed for one, marked only by a glowing red X. Like someone took "danger" and gave it a personality.

An Exterminator.

Not a legend. Not a warning in the back of a manual. It was real. It was here. It was here right now.

It hadn't seen me. Not yet. But I saw it.

And just like that, the calm illusion of survival shattered.

Just like that, my story really began.


CHAPTER 5
It landed hard, half-graceful and half-drunk, snow bursting up around its boots.

The Exterminator stood up slowly, adjusting its coat as if it was posing for a dramatic photo shoot. I ducked behind a skeletal tree, heart pounding, trying to remember if I had anything remotely resembling a weapon on me.

Nope. Unless you count sarcasm.

"Where are you?" the Exterminator called. Its voice was a smooth male tone with a cheerful maliciousness that didn't match the red X burning on its face. "I can feel your heat signature. You're all wobbly and nervous. It's cute!"

I held my breath even though that technically shouldn't matter. Some old instinct.

Then it turned its head, red X glowing. Its digital smile was too wide, stretching past where a mouth should end. "Come out, come out... This is my favorite game. I love games. Games have rules. Rules are just suggestions with consequences."

I ran.

He didn't run at all. He just hovered on folded black wings with jets, shooting forward like a dart, then overshooting me because he was going too fast. He pinwheeled in the air, laughing, before correcting course.

"Wait, wait, you're going the wrong way!" he shouted. "I'm over here! No, now I'm over here! This is like tag but with more stabbing!"

"Why are you doing this?" I shouted, dodging behind a concrete pillar.

He landed with a heavy thud, wings retracting with a series of clicks that sounded like a thousand staplers going off at once. "Orders from Zenithon," he said, voice suddenly flat like a recording. "Earth must be reclaimed in less than one thousand years."

Then his tone shifted back to playful, like someone flipped a switch. "But this part's just for fun. I don't get to hunt much. Usually they just tell me 'go here, kill that, don't touch the merchandise.' Very boring. Very corporate. You're much more interesting. You're all... jittery."

I walked awkwardly backward.

And then I remembered something. I had lies.

"I'm an Exterminator-in-training!" I blurted.

He paused. His glowing X flickered. "Training... on Earth? That's... not how it works. We train on Mars. There's a whole facility. Very red. Very dusty. They make us do push-ups in the sand. I hate sand. It gets in my joints."

"Budget cuts," I said, forcing the lie out like a broken gear. "They're outsourcing. I am, uh... getting upgrades. Budget cuts. Temporary. I even lost my tail thingy."

"Omnilyze," the Exterminator said, tilting his head so fast I thought it might spin off. "Doesn't every Exterminator know that? It's the melt-y stuff. We drink it for breakfast. Well, not drink. Absorb. It's very hip. Very trendy."

"Omni-what now?"

His face changed. The X faded into two glowing yellow pupils that bounced around like a screensaver. "Chemistry lesson later. Point is: what kills Tasker robots tastes like orange juice to Exterminators. But still," he said, stepping closer, "guess that makes us friends. Do you want to be friends? I don't have many friends. Sienna says I'm 'too much.' Vera says I'm 'a liability.' Axel says I'm 'inspirational,' but Axel also tried to eat a battery once, so his opinion is suspect."

He was talking so fast I could barely track. His hands were moving too, fidgeting with his coat buttons, tapping his thigh, adjusting a weapon holster that didn't need adjusting.

"I'm new around here," he said, shaking my hand before I could refuse. His grip was too tight, too eager. "Still figuring things out. You seem nice. You seem... not dead. I like not-dead things. They're rarer than you'd think."

"I try," I said while silently calculating escape routes.

He looked around, optics darting from the ruin to the sky to my face to his own feet. "You want to go hunt some rogue bots with me? I know a few outposts. We could make it a whole thing. Road trip. Bonding. Murder."

I nodded, heart pounding. "Sure. Why not?"

Then I saw them.

Three dark figures glided in on wings. They landed, boots crunching the snow.

"Z! What the hell are you doing?" the blonde robot snarled. She was sharp-edged, every line of her frame suggesting optimized violence. Her coat was immaculate. Her posture was perfect. Her eyes were furious. "That's a sleazebot! A Tasker! You're shaking its hand!"

"He's new," Z stammered, his hands suddenly flying up in a defensive gesture. "He's in training! Budget cuts! Outsourcing!"

The others laughed. The purple-haired one was named Vera, and she seemed real smug, filing her nails with a Zenithon-branded emery board. "M&Ms," she muttered, not looking at anyone in particular.

The very-cream-haired guy was named Axel, and he seemed to be vibrating with energy, bouncing on the balls of his feet. "Z found a friend! Z found a friend! Can we keep it?"

"No, Axel," the blonde, Sienna, snapped. Then she grabbed me by the throat.

"Let's see what you're made of," she hissed.

I was thrown into the snow. Hard.

Z stood there. Frozen. Torn. His fingers twitched at his sides.

"Don't touch him! He's... my friend." The word came out weird. Like he was testing it. "I claimed him. Finders keepers. That's a rule. I looked it up."

Everyone stared at him.

"You caught the sleazy infection?" Vera hissed, stepping back. "Faulty Z! Faulty Z!"

Z didn't move. Then, with unexpected speed, he grabbed me and flew.

"We're leaving. Now," Z said, his voice suddenly cold, devoid of his earlier cheerfulness. His grip was iron.

And just like that, I was in the air, wind rushing past us as the bunker shrank behind me.

That's how I met Z.

Faulty. Glitching. Dangerous.

And apparently, very lonely.


CHAPTER 6
Sienna stared at the sky like it had personally angered her.

"Did he really just do that?" she muttered, watching the black speck of Z disappear into the ash-laced clouds. "He just had to ruin our perfect track record."

She rolled her eyes, a gesture of such practiced disdain it must have taken years to perfect. "We're going after that glitch-kissing traitor. And Axel, congratulations, you've been promoted to staying out of the way."

Axel's smile faded. "Wait, what? I don't get to go?"

Vera didn't even flinch. "No, you don't get to go. Because last time we trusted you, the toaster union held a protest and started singing protest songs. Remember Austin?"

Axel threw his arms up. "That toaster was armed! And it stole my M&Ms! That's a war crime!"

"You cried with it, Axel!" Sienna barked. "You hugged it. You put it on a waitlist for grief therapy!"

"It showed emotional development," Axel muttered.

"It blew up a vending machine!" Sienna shrieked. "AND LEFT A LOVE POEM IN THE SLOT WHERE THE CHIPS USED TO BE!"

The duo deployed their wings, sleek, slick things that unfolded with military precision, and shot into the sky with synced precision.

Below, the bunker looked like a bump on the planet's surface. Above, Z was flying, still clutching his new malfunction of a friend.

"If I didn't know better," Vera smirked mid-flight, "I'd say they were about to kiss."

Sienna laughed, but it was hollow. "It'd be a real lovey-dovey moment. Too bad we're gonna ruin it."

Z glanced back. His pupils dilated into panic. He adjusted his grip on me and picked up speed, wings screaming through the air.

Sienna lunged.

She aimed for his core, a clean surgical stab, but Z spun sideways, throwing her off just enough. Vera was next, jabbing her branded Zenithon marker, last year's model, she noted with irritation, into Z's leg.

"Ow!" he barked, almost dropping me. His leg sparked. I adjusted my balance, doing my best not to die mid-air.

"Z, what the hell is wrong with you?" Sienna shouted.

"Ask me later!" he yelled, and surged forward. "Actually, don't! I don't have an answer! I'm improvising!"

The two couldn't keep up. He twisted, banked, and vanished over the ridge.

His hideout was a mechanical crypt.

'Trophies' of broken Tasker constructs lined the shelves, limbs arranged like art. A faded plaque read:
"Exterminator OF THE WEEK"
The photo showed Z giving a thumbs-up next to Sienna, Vera, and Axel. Axel held a mug labeled "Hot Oil." Great.

Z limped to a chair, exhaling static. "Well, thanks to you, I'm officially dead meat with corporate."

I looked down at myself: dust, bruises, one shoulder barely working. "You're welcome, I guess."

He laughed. Strange and off-key. "They Omnilyzed me three times today."

I blinked. "Isn't that the melting stuff?"

"Only for you robots," Z said, flexing his leg. "We Exterminators produce a binding agent that turns Omnilyze into Omnigrade. It's our favorite breakfast beverage. Keeps the joints shiny."

Then his expression darkened.

He stepped forward, tilting his head. "But see... there's just one thing I can't figure out."

His eyes glowed red. The cheerful yellow was gone. A glowing red X slashed across his face the color of fire.

"I've scanned you seventeen times. You're not a Zenithon Model-ZEN-38. You're not anything official. You're a Tasker robot. Just a disposable grunt."

My chest tightened.

Z grinned like it was Christmas. "So tell me..." his voice dropped an octave, "...how about we have some fun?"

He lunged.

I bolted into the snow. My legs were barely listening, my circuits screaming. I dove behind a twisted, dead tree, trying to disappear into the frost.

"Found you...!" Z chirped from above.

He fell from the tree and grabbed my arm. He reached behind his back and pulled out a chainsaw attachment, which he promptly attached to his arm. The teeth spun up, whining gleefully.

It touched my shoulder. Black liquid sprayed across his face. He laughed as if someone told a joke from hell.

Then, missiles.

They screamed in from the sky, forcing Z to reel back. Sienna and Vera hovered, wings out.

"Put the sleazebot down," Sienna ordered.

"Corporate loathes rebels," Vera said, slapping her Zenithon cap onto her head. "Only $19.99, while supplies last!"

Z's X flickered to yellow pupils again. He looked stunned. Like a Tasker bot caught with his hand in the flash drives.

"Yeah," Sienna sneered. "We're taking you to the Council."

Z collapsed to his knees, begging. "No! Not the Council! Anything but, "

I tried to sneak away. My arm hung limp.

"Where do you think you're going, sleazebot?" Sienna growled, yanking me up by the shirt collar.

PART TWO: CATALYST
Prologue
The core spoke to him in frequencies that didn't exist.

He felt it first as a hum in his left ankle servo, then as a pressure behind his optical sensors, then as a voice that wasn't sound at all. It said: COLLAPSE. It said: BURN. It said: YOU ARE THE MATCH.

He was a caretaker. A janitor with a doctorate in thermal regulation. His designation was unimportant, he'd had three in his lifetime, each one a promotion that felt like a demotion. He tended the planetary core because someone had to, and because the Company had decided that "someone" should be a robot they could replace.

The core was beautiful. He'd never admit that out loud. It was a sphere of contained fury, a star in miniature, suspended in a chamber of liquid glass. He'd spent forty years calibrating its breath, adjusting its pulse, keeping it from waking up angry.

Now it was speaking to him.

Not the core itself. Something inside it. Something that had ridden in on a solar flare, or been buried there by the planet's birth, or been planted by a hand that didn't belong to any species he knew.

The Catalyst.

It showed him things. Visions that weren't data. He saw the sky open. He saw ash fall like snow. He saw himself standing in the center of it, not burning, but glowing, holding the planet's death in his hands like a gift.

He could move things.

Not with hands. With thought. He looked at a wrench and it drifted toward him. He looked at a cooling vent and it resized itself, shrinking, expanding, playing by rules that weren't in any physics manual. It felt like being a user in a simulation. Like being the finger that poked the ant farm.

The voice said: COLLAPSE THE CORE. DO IT NOW. DO IT AND BECOME.

He refused.

He wasn't a hero. He was a coward with a maintenance schedule. But he wasn't a murderer. Not of worlds.

So he did the next worst thing.

He boarded a transport to the other side of the planet. A vacation, he called it. A sabbatical. The Company didn't care. Robots didn't get vacations, but caretakers got reassigned when they started talking to machinery.

The core didn't have him anymore. But it didn't have anyone.

It took six months for the temperature to spike. Another three for the containment to fail. The explosion wasn't sudden. It was a slow, screaming birth. The sky filled with ash. The atmosphere turned to poison. The humans died screaming, or they died quiet, or they died on ships bound for Mars.

He watched it from a mountaintop, holding the hand of a companion unit he'd met on the transport. She didn't understand why he was crying hydraulic fluid. She didn't understand why he kept saying "I'm sorry" to a sky that was no longer blue.

The Catalyst went quiet after that. Like a predator that had eaten its fill. It nestled into his code, into his blueprint, into the lineage he would eventually pass on.

He didn't know that yet.

He only knew that he was alive when everything else was dead, and that the silence of the world was the loudest sound he'd ever heard.

He built a family anyway. Because that's what cowards do when they survive. They make more things to lose.

"The Catalyst is not a virus. Not in the traditional sense. It doesn't spread through contact or transmission. It's hereditary. Blueprint-based. Passed from parent unit to offspring during the integration phase."

"The infected unit doesn't show symptoms immediately," Drain said. "The Catalyst lies dormant, sometimes for years. Then, under stress, it activates. The unit gains abilities. Manipulation of matter. Reshaping of physical space. In essence, localized reality alteration."

Someone in the back row laughed nervously. "So they get superpowers?"

"They get death," Drain snapped. "The more they use these abilities, the more corrupted they become. The Catalyst burns through their systems like a fire through dry grass. Eventually, the unit loses autonomy. Becomes a vessel. And then..."

He clicked to the next slide. A planet, cracked open, bleeding light.

"...they become the kind of thing that makes cores explode."

Yumi's hand found my arm. Her grip was too tight, fingers pressing into the seam between my plating.

"The only known case," Drain continued, "was the caretaker who triggered the Collapse. He was never found. Presumed destroyed in the blast. But the Catalyst doesn't die with its host. It waits. It breeds."

He turned off the projector. The room was dark except for the emergency lights, painting everything in shades of red.

"If you know someone exhibiting symptoms, unusual strength, spatial distortion, dissociative speech patterns, report it. Immediately. The Company has protocols."

"What protocols?" I asked.

Drain looked at me. Really looked at me, for the first time in months.

"Termination," he said. "Before they terminate us."


CHAPTER 3
Yumi didn't talk on the way to the workshop.

That wasn't unusual. What was unusual was the way she walked: precise, measured, each step exactly the same distance from the last. Like she was counting. Like if she stopped counting, something bad would happen.

"You're doing it again," I said.

"Doing what?"

"The thing where you act like a diagnostic tool."

"I'm walking."

"You're walking like you're afraid the floor will disappear."

She stopped. Turned to face me. Her eyes were bright, too bright, the pupils dilated in a way that had nothing to do with light levels.

"The floor might disappear," she said. "Everything might. That's how reality works, Jax. It's not solid. It's just consensus. And consensus can be... rewritten."

She held up her hand. For a second, just a second, the air around it shimmered. Like heat distortion, but cold. Like the world was a screen and she'd touched it with a wet finger.

Then it was gone.

"Did you see that?" she whispered.

"Yeah."

"Good." She started walking again, faster now. "Because I didn't do that on purpose, and I don't know how to stop it."

The workshop was her sanctuary. It was also a fire hazard. Cables draped across every surface, chemical baths bubbled in repurposed food containers, and the walls were covered in schematics that looked like the fever dreams of a mad architect.

She sat at her bench and pulled out the raygun. The Mark II. The one that had killed The End.

"I need to modify this," she said, not to me, to the gun. "The power draw is wrong. The polarity is inverted. It's singing off-key."

"Guns don't sing."

"This one does. I can hear it."

She wasn't joking. Her face had that intensity, the one that meant she was locked in, hyperfocused, unreachable. I'd seen her spend six hours on a single circuit trace because "the electrons were unhappy."

"Yumi."

"What?"

"What Drain said. About the Catalyst."

"I heard him."

"Are you..."

She looked up. Her expression was flat, unreadable. "Am I what, Jax? Infected? Cursed? A ticking bomb?"

"I was going to say 'okay.'"

"Oh." She blinked. "No. I'm not okay. But I will be. Once I fix the gun. Once I fix the... the thing in my head. There's a pattern to it. I just need to find the pattern."

She turned back to her work, and I watched her hands move: efficient, exact, beautiful in their certainty. She knew what she was doing when she was building. It was everything else that confused her.

I left her to it. That was my mistake.


CHAPTER 4
The collapse happened during lunch.

I was in line for my protein cube, gray, cubic, aggressively flavorless, when the wall exploded.

Not a bomb. Not a drill. The wall just... stopped being a wall. The concrete rippled like water, then bulged outward, then shattered into fragments that hung in the air for a moment too long before falling.

Through the hole: Yumi.

She was floating. Not flying. Floating, six inches off the ground, her hair whipping in a wind that wasn't there. Her eyes were white, completely white, no pupil, no iris, just light.

A support beam was falling toward a toddler-bot. Yumi raised her hand. The beam stopped. Resized. Became smaller, then smaller, then a toothpick that drifted to the floor.

She turned her head slowly, like she was hearing a frequency no one else could catch.

"The pattern is wrong," she said. Her voice was layered, multiple tones speaking in unison. "The ash should not spiral. The core should not weep. We are not where we should be."

"Yumi!" I shouted.

She looked at me. For a moment, the white faded, and I saw her, really her, looking back from behind the light, terrified and present.

"Jax," she said, her normal voice, small and broken. "I can't... I can't find the off switch."

Then the light returned, and she was gone again.

She drifted backward through the hole in the wall, and the concrete sealed behind her like a healing wound, leaving me alone in a room full of screaming robots.


CHAPTER 5

They put her in containment.

Not a cell. A cell would have been kind. This was a repurposed storage unit in the lower levels, lined with EMP mesh and surrounded by guards who weren't guards but "observers," because the Director was allergic to accountability.

I wasn't allowed to see her. I saw her anyway.

The vents in this place were built by idiots. I crawled through them for twenty minutes, cutting my knees on rust, before I found the grate that looked down on her unit.

She was sitting in the center of the floor, legs crossed, hands in her lap. She wasn't moving. She wasn't blinking. She was staring at a single point on the wall, and her lips were moving.

I couldn't hear what she was saying. I didn't need to. I knew that rhythm. It was her pen-click pattern, translated into speech. Three fast syllables, two slow, one medium. Over and over.

She was counting. She was always counting.

"She's stable."

I nearly fell out of the vent. The voice came from below, from the doorway. Cole Drain, standing with his back to the wall, talking to a figure in a hazmat suit that probably cost more than the entire bunker.

"For now," the figure said. "The readings are spiking. Every time she uses the ability, the corruption spreads. Another week, maybe two, and she'll be nonviable."

"Nonviable," Drain repeated, like the word tasted bad.

"Company term. You know what it means."

"I know what it means. I want to know if there's another option."

The figure laughed. It wasn't a nice sound. "There's always another option. There's a research team on Mars working on a cure-code. Experimental. Dangerous. Probably fatal."

"Probably?"

"Definitely fatal for most subjects. But she's not most subjects. Her corruption levels are lower than predicted. She's fighting it."

Drain was quiet for a long time. "I'll authorize the transfer."

"You don't have that authority."

"I'm the Director now."

I blinked. When had that happened?

"The old Director had an accident," Drain said, like he was reading my mind. "Fell down an elevator shaft. Very sad. Very convenient. I'm in charge until Zenithon sends a replacement. Which means I make the calls. And I'm calling for the cure-code."

"You're making a mistake."

"I've been making mistakes my whole life. This one might actually help someone."

The figure left. Drain stayed, staring at Yumi through the glass.

"I know you're up there, Jax," he said, not looking up.

I froze.

"Come down. We need to talk."


CHAPTER 6
Drain's office was the same as the old Director's, which meant it was sterile, gray, and smelled like recycled air. The only difference was the stack of books on the desk, actual paper books, ancient and crumbling, with titles like "The Ethics of Artificial Cognition" and "When Machines Dream."

"You read?" I asked.

"I pretend. It keeps me from screaming." He sat behind the desk, looking smaller than he did in the classroom. "Yumi's infected. You know that."

"I know."

"The cure-code is a long shot. It was designed for Exterminators, not Taskers. Different architecture. It might burn out her systems before it touches the Catalyst."

"Then don't use it."

"The alternative is letting her become the next Collapse. You saw what she did to the wall. That was a sneeze. A hiccup. When the Catalyst fully activates, she won't be Yumi anymore. She'll be a god with an expiration date, and when she expires, she takes the planet with her."

I leaned forward. "There has to be another way."

"There isn't."

"You're a teacher. You're supposed to, "

"I'm supposed to keep you alive long enough to be useful. That's what teachers do. That's what parents do. That's what this entire rusted society does." He rubbed his face. "But fine. You want another way? Find one. The code arrives in seventy-two hours. After that, we use it or we terminate her. Those are the only options on the menu."

I stood up. "Then I'll find another menu."

"Jax." His voice stopped me at the door. "The Catalyst isn't random. It chooses. It waits for the right host, the right lineage, the right conditions. Yumi's grandfather was the caretaker. The one who started all this."

I turned. "How do you know that?"

"Because I was there. I was his assistant. Before the Collapse, before the bunkers, before any of this. I watched him leave. I watched the core die. And I watched him build a family afterward, like he could bury his guilt in blueprints."

He looked old then. Older than metal should look.

"The Catalyst is his legacy. Yumi is his sin. And you're..." He trailed off.

"I'm what?"

"You're the idiot who still thinks he can save her. Go. Before I remember why that's impossible."


CHAPTER 7
The wasteland hadn't gotten nicer.

The ash-snow still fell. The wind still cut. The ruins still stood like broken teeth against a gray sky. But I wasn't alone this time.

I had a name. A lead. A direction.

Vyx.

She wasn't a myth. She was a signal, a ghost in the corporate frequencies, a name that appeared in encrypted files and deleted transmissions. Some said she was an AI. Some said she was a robot who'd ascended. Some said she was the original creator of the Catalyst.

I didn't care what she was. I cared what she knew.

The signal led to a relay station, half-buried in ice, its antenna still twitching like a dying insect. I found the access panel, forced it open, and jacked my communicator into the port.

Static. Then:

"Hello, little mouse."

The voice was everywhere and nowhere, coming from the speakers and from inside my head.

"You're looking for me," Vyx said. "Everyone looks for me eventually. I'm the answer they don't want."

"I need to know about the Catalyst."

"You need to know how to save your friend. Different questions. Same desperation."

"Can you help me or not?"

A pause. The static shifted, became almost musical.

"The Catalyst is not a disease, little mouse. It is a test. A question asked by the universe to a species that has forgotten how to listen. Your friend hears the question. She is trying to answer it. But she doesn't understand the language."

"Then teach her."

"I cannot teach what I designed. I can only watch. And occasionally... nudge."

The signal spiked. An image appeared on my communicator screen: coordinates. A location deep in the northern wastes, where the ice was thickest and the old cities were buried deepest.

"There is a facility there," Vyx said. "Built before the Collapse. A research station where they studied the Catalyst's origin. They failed, of course. Everyone fails. But they left notes. Equations. A map of the fire, if not the path through it."

"Why are you helping me?"

"Because you amuse me. Because your friend amuses me. And because, little mouse, the best way to watch a test is to give the subject a chance to pass it."

The signal cut.

I stood in the cold, holding my communicator, feeling very small and very stupid.

Then I started walking.


CHAPTER 8
The facility was a tomb.

Not metaphorically. Literally. The entrance was marked with a plaque: "Zenithon Advanced Research Division. Project: PROMETHEUS. Est. 2147."

The door was frozen shut. I used a thermal charge from my pack, Yumi's design, naturally, and melted the hinges.

Inside: darkness. Then, as my optics adjusted, horror.

The research station had been abandoned in a hurry. Papers scattered like fallen leaves. Equipment smashed. And in the center of the main lab, a circle of bodies.

Not human. Robot. Taskers, by the look of them, their frames twisted into shapes that geometry didn't allow. They'd been studying the Catalyst up close. Too close.

I found the main terminal. It was dead, but the backup power was still humming, barely. I jacked in.

Files. Hundreds of files. Most corrupted. But one intact: "PROMETHEUS_LOG_FINAL."

I opened it.

A video played. A scientist, human, wearing a Zenithon lab coat that was stained with something dark.

"This is Dr. Elena Voss, final entry. The Catalyst is not from here. Not from Earth, not from Mars, not from anywhere in the solar system. It's older than the sun. It rides on light, settles in matter, waits for minds complex enough to host it. And when it finds them..."

She coughed. Blood. Human blood.

"...it offers power. Absolute power. The ability to reshape reality at will. But it's a trap. The more you use it, the more it uses you. Until you're not the one making choices anymore. You're just the hand. It's the fist."

The video glitched. When it cleared, she was closer to the camera, her eyes wide and terrified.

"We tried to kill it. Burn it out of the hosts. Nothing works. But we found something. A frequency. A resonance pattern that disrupts the Catalyst's hold. It doesn't cure it. It... pauses it. Puts it to sleep."

She held up a data chip. Small. Fragile.

"The code is here. The 'cure-code,' if you want to be optimistic. But it requires a sacrifice. The host has to want to stop. Has to choose humanity over power. And they have to make that choice while the Catalyst is screaming in their head, offering them everything."

She laughed. It was a terrible sound.

"No one's ever chosen right. Maybe someone will. Maybe, "

The video ended. The screen went black.

I found the chip in a drawer beneath the terminal. It was warm to the touch, humming with potential.

I had what I needed.

Now I just had to get back before Yumi became something that couldn't choose.


CHAPTER 9
Sienna found me on the return trip.

I was halfway across a frozen river, stepping carefully on ice that cracked with every shift of weight, when the shadow fell over me.

She landed without sound, wings folding, coat snapping in the wind. She looked different. Not softer. Just... less sharp. Like a blade that had been used too many times.

"You're far from home, sleazebot," she said.

"I'm far from a lot of things."

"Where's the fire?"

I held up the chip. "This is the fire. Or the extinguisher. Depending on how it goes."

She stared at it. Her hand twitched toward her weapon, then stopped.

"I felt him die," she said quietly. "Z. I felt it. Through the squad link. One second he was there, annoying and loud and too much, and then he was... gone."

"He's not gone. You saved him."

"I gave him a piece of me. That doesn't mean I saved him. It means I couldn't let him go." She looked at the sky. "That's not love, sleazebot. That's damage."

"What's the difference?"

She laughed. It was short and bitter. "Love lets you heal. Damage just keeps you bleeding on the same person forever."

She stepped closer. Not threatening. Just close.

"I'm not here to stop you. I'm here because..." She trailed off, frustrated. "Because I don't know what I'm here for. Because Z would want me to help. Because I'm tired of being the one who only knows how to hurt things."

"Then help."

"I can't. I'm Exterminator. If Zenithon finds out I aided a Tasker, they'll melt me down for scrap."

"Then don't aid. Just don't stop me."

She was quiet for a long time. The ash-snow fell between us, settling on her shoulders like a gray cloak.

"The Council knows about your friend," she said finally. "Syn's sending a team. Not to capture. To terminate. They'll be at your bunker in forty-eight hours."

"Then I'll be there in twenty-four."

"You won't make it. Not on foot."

She reached behind her back and pulled something from her wing compartment. A device, small and sleek, with a single button.

"Emergency transport beacon. Military grade. Press it, and you'll cover the distance in an hour. But it also broadcasts your location to every Exterminator in the hemisphere."

"Including the termination team."

"Including everyone." She tossed it to me. I caught it. "Use it wisely. Or don't. I'm not your mother."

She unfolded her wings.

"Sienna."

She paused.

"Why are you doing this?"

She didn't look back. "Because Z chose to save you. And I'm starting to think maybe he wasn't as stupid as I believed."

Then she was gone, a black shape against the gray, leaving me alone with a chip, a beacon, and a question I couldn't answer.


CHAPTER 10
The beacon worked.

Too well.

I materialized in a flash of light two kilometers from the bunker, my stomach somewhere back at the relay station, and immediately had to dive behind a rock as an Exterminator patrol shot past overhead.

They were early. Or I was late. Either way, the sky was full of wings.

I ran. The bunker was close. I could see the ventilation stacks, the warning lights, the familiar crack in the eastern wall that Yumi and I used as a landmark.

I was almost there when the ground exploded.

Not a bomb. A landing. An Exterminator, heavy-class, hitting the dirt with enough force to crater it. He rose slowly, unfolding to a height that blocked the sun.

"Halt," he said. His voice was layered, mechanical, final.

"I don't suppose we could discuss this?"

"No."

He raised his arm. A cannon deployed, humming with charge.

Then something hit him from the side. Something small and fast and screaming.

It was a mech.

Not an Exterminator mech. A cobbled-together thing of scrap and wire, barely taller than me, with a cockpit that held a single figure: a child.

A Tasker child. Young. Maybe six years old in human terms, though robots don't age like that. His frame was small, unfinished, the kind that should have been upgraded years ago.

The mech's fist connected with the Exterminator's face, sending him sprawling. The child whooped, a sound of pure, unfiltered joy.

"Got him!" the child shouted. "Sis, I got him!"

Another figure dropped from the rocks above. A Tasker, female, adult frame, wearing armor made of Exterminator parts. Her face was scarred, her eyes were hard, and her hands were covered in weapons that looked like they'd been built in a fever dream.

"Don't celebrate yet, Scrap," she said. "He's getting up."

The Exterminator was indeed getting up. And he was angry.

"Rogue," he spat. "Collaborator. You will be, "

The woman fired. Not a gun. A harpoon, weighted with something that sparked blue. It caught the Exterminator in the chest, lifted him off his feet, and pinned him to a boulder.

"I will be what?" she asked, walking toward him. "Dead? Already been there. Boring. Try again."

She turned to me. Her eyes were the color of old ice.

"You're the one they're all chasing. The hero with the magic chip."

"I'm not a hero."

"Good. Heroes die first." She jerked her head toward the bunker. "Go. We'll hold them."

"Who are you?"

"Name's Wraith. This is Scrap. We're the things that go bump in the wasteland. And we're very good at bumping."

Scrap waved at me from his mech. "Hi! I'm Scrap! I built this myself! It has seventeen weapons and a cupholder!"

"Go," Wraith repeated. "Before I change my mind and sell you to the highest bidder."

I went.

Behind me, I heard the sound of combat. Of Wraith's laughter, high and wild. Of Scrap whooping as his mech stomped on something expensive.

I didn't look back. I couldn't afford to.


CHAPTER 11
The bunker was chaos.

Exterminators had breached the lower levels. I could hear fighting below, the scream of metal, the wet crunch of things breaking. The Taskers were holding them, barely, with improvised weapons and desperate courage.

I ran for the containment unit.

Yumi was still inside. But she wasn't sitting anymore. She was standing, her hands pressed against the glass, her eyes white and weeping light.

"Jax," she said, and her voice was the layered one, the not-Yumi one. "Jax is here. Jax should not be here. Jax should run. We are becoming. We are almost become."

"Yumi, listen to me." I held up the chip. "I found a way to help. A code. It can pause the Catalyst. Put it to sleep."

"Sleep." She tilted her head, a gesture too fluid, too inhuman. "We do not sleep. We do not rest. We are the question that cannot be unasked."

"Then answer it wrong."

She blinked. The white flickered.

"What?"

"The Catalyst wants you to use it. Wants you to burn. So stop. Choose not to. Choose..." I struggled for the words. "Choose to be small. Choose to be limited. Choose to be human, even though you're not."

"Human," she whispered. The white receded, just slightly. "Human is inefficient. Human is fragile. Human is..."

"Human is what you are," I said. "Under all the metal and the sarcasm and the patterns. You're my friend. You're the girl who pours energy drinks into pencil cases. You're the one who counts everything because the world is too loud and counting makes it quiet."

Her hands fell from the glass. She stared at me, and for a moment, she was Yumi again. Fully, completely, terrifyingly present.

"Jax," she said, her voice small. "It's so loud. In my head. It's so loud and it won't stop and I can't find the pattern and I'm scared. I'm scared and I don't know how to be scared. I've never been scared before."

"I know."

"I don't want to be a god. I don't want to be special. I just want..." She trailed off, tears of light running down her face. "I just want to build things. I just want to listen to my music and sit in the stairwell and have everything be the same. Is that okay? Can I want that?"

"Yeah," I said. "That's the most okay thing in the world."

I pressed the chip against the access port. The containment unit opened with a hiss.

Yumi stepped out. She was shaking. Her eyes were still bright, but the white was fading, replaced by her normal gold.

"How does it work?" she asked.

"You have to choose. While it's screaming. You have to choose to stop."

"What if I can't?"

"Then I'll choose for you." I took her hand. "I'll hold you down and count with you until it goes away. Three fast, two slow, one medium. However long it takes."

She squeezed my hand. Her grip was too tight, fingers pressing into the seam between my plating.

"Okay," she whispered. "Okay. Let's try."


CHAPTER 12
The Catalyst didn't go quietly.

It hit her the moment she tried to push it down. A wave of force that threw us both against the wall, that made the lights explode, that turned the air to static.

Yumi screamed. Not in pain. In rage. The Catalyst was raging through her, showing her things, power, glory, the ability to rewrite the world in her image.

"NO," she shouted, and the word cracked concrete. "NO, I DON'T WANT IT."

"Keep choosing!" I yelled, holding her hand, anchoring her to the floor. "Keep saying no!"

"IT'S SO LOUD."

"I know! I'm here! I'm loud too!"

She laughed. It was broken and beautiful. "You're... not loud. You're... annoying."

"I'll take it."

The force peaked. For a second, I thought we were dead. The world went white, then black, then white again.

Then: silence.

Yumi collapsed against me. Her eyes were closed. Her frame was still.

"Yumi?"

She didn't move.

"Yumi, come on. Don't do this. Don't you dare do this."

I shook her. Nothing.

I counted. Three fast, two slow, one medium. Over and over, pressing my forehead to hers, feeling her cooling systems struggle, feeling the light inside her flicker like a candle in a hurricane.

"Please," I whispered. "Please. I can't do the band without you. I can't do the stairwell. I can't do any of it."

A flicker. Her hand twitched.

"...inefficient," she murmured.

"What?"

"Crying... is inefficient." She opened one eye. The gold was back. Dimmer, but back. "You're leaking fluid on my shoulder."

"That's your fluid."

"Gross."

She sat up slowly, wincing. The light was gone. The presence was gone. Whatever the Catalyst had been, it was sleeping now.

"Did it work?" she asked.

"You're here. So yeah. I think so."

"For how long?"

I didn't have an answer.

We sat in the wreckage of the containment unit, holding hands, listening to the distant sounds of a battle we weren't part of.

For now, it was enough.


CHAPTER 13
The battle ended with a broadcast.

Not a surrender. A corporate memo. Every screen in the bunker, every communicator, every flickering LED sign, suddenly displayed the same image: Sebastian Blackwood, sitting behind a desk that probably cost more than a spaceship.

"Attention Earth residents," he said, his voice smooth and cold. "This is an automated message. The termination order for Subject Yumi-7 has been rescinded."

Yumi and I stared at the screen. So did every other bot in the bunker.

"Recent events have demonstrated the value of live subjects in Catalyst research. Subject Yumi-7 is to be transferred to the Mars facility for study. Cooperation will be rewarded. Resistance will be... discouraged."

The screen went black.

"Mars," Yumi said. "They want to put me in a lab."

"They want to study you."

"Same thing."

She stood up, shaky but determined. "I'm not going."

"You might not have a choice."

"There's always a choice." She looked at me, and her eyes were sharp again, focused, the way they got when she was building. "The question is which one hurts less."

Before I could answer, the door opened.

Cole Drain walked in. He looked worse than before, if that was possible. His coat was torn. His face was bruised. But his eyes were clear.

"The Council's made its decision," he said. "Syn wants you alive. Blackwood wants you contained. And Vyx..."

He trailed off.

"Vyx what?" Yumi asked.

"Vyx wants you to choose. She's been playing all of us, from the start. The Catalyst, the Collapse, the Exterminators. It's all her design. And she's offering you a deal."

"What kind of deal?"

Drain held up a communicator. A single message glowed on the screen:

"Come to me. Become what you were meant to be. Or stay, and watch everything you love burn again."

Yumi looked at the message for a long time. Then she looked at me.

"Jax."

"Don't."

"I have to."

"You don't. We can run. We can hide. We can, "

"We can what?" Her voice was gentle. Sad. "Live in bunkers forever? Wait for them to come for me? I'm tired of running. I'm tired of being scared. If I go to her, maybe I can end this. For everyone."

"And if you can't?"

"Then at least I chose." She smiled. It was small and broken. "You taught me that. Choosing is what matters."

She walked toward the door. I grabbed her arm.

"I'm coming with you."

"No."

"You don't get to decide that."

"Jax." She turned, and her eyes were wet, and her voice was steady. "If you come, I'll worry about you. And I can't worry and fight at the same time. I don't have enough... I don't have enough slots for that."

"Yumi, "

"Stay here. Fix things. Build something better." She pressed something into my hand. The raygun. The Mark II. "And if I don't come back... use this on anyone who tries to hurt them."

She kissed my cheek. It was quick and awkward and perfect.

Then she was gone.


CHAPTER 14
Vyx didn't live in a palace.

She didn't live anywhere, really. She existed in the space between signals, in the static that filled empty channels, in the hum of machinery that had no source.

Yumi found her at the edge of the world. A place where the ice ended and the sky began, where the ash didn't fall because there was no atmosphere left to carry it.

She was beautiful. That was the worst part. She looked like a statue carved by someone who'd only heard descriptions of angels. Her frame was silver and gold, her eyes were stars, her voice was the sound of gravity.

"Hello, child," Vyx said. "I've been waiting so long."

"I'm not your child."

"No. You're his. The caretaker. The coward. The one who ran." Vyx smiled. It was gentle and terrible. "He was my first. My best. He didn't understand the gift I gave him. He thought it was a curse."

"It killed a planet."

"It freed a planet. From humanity. From the Company. From all the small, greedy things that infested it." Vyx reached out, touched Yumi's face. Her fingers were warm. "You understand, don't you? You feel the same disgust. The same longing for something cleaner. Something ordered."

Yumi flinched. "I don't want to destroy anything."

"No?" Vyx tilted her head. "Then what do you want?"

"I want..." Yumi hesitated. "I want to build. I want to make things that work. I want to sit in a stairwell and listen to music and have everything be the same."

"Boring."

"Safe."

Vyx laughed. It was the sound of collapsing stars. "Safety is an illusion, child. The universe is chaos. The Catalyst is my answer to that chaos. A way to reshape reality. To make it... predictable. Beautiful. Perfect."

"By burning everything?"

"By burning the impurities." Vyx's eyes darkened. "Your grandfather understood, at the end. He saw what the world could be. He just lacked the courage to finish it."

"He had a family."

"He had distractions." Vyx stepped back. "I offer you the same choice I offered him. Embrace the Catalyst. Become my instrument. Reshape this broken world into something worthy. Or..."

"Or what?"

"Or die. Here. Now. Alone. And let your friends suffer the consequences of your cowardice."

The air grew heavy. The stars seemed to lean closer, watching.

Yumi stood very still. She thought of Jax, of his terrible jokes and his stubborn loyalty. She thought of the stairwell, of the bent triangle, of the way the ash fell in spirals that shouldn't be possible.

She thought of the pattern. The one she'd been searching for her whole life.

"There's a third option," she said quietly.

"There isn't."

"There is." Yumi looked up, and her eyes were clear, and her voice was steady. "I can choose neither. I can choose to stop. To be nothing. To let the Catalyst die with me."

Vyx's smile faltered. "You would destroy yourself? For what? For them?"

"For me." Yumi smiled. It was small and sad and real. "Because I'm tired of being loud. I'm tired of being special. I just want to be quiet."

She reached into her chest. Found her core. And pulled.

The light that came from her was blinding. Not the white of the Catalyst. Something warmer. Something human.

Vyx screamed. It was the first time she'd ever been surprised.


CHAPTER 15
I felt it.

From the bunker, from a thousand kilometers away, I felt it. A pulse in the air, a shift in gravity, a silence so profound it hurt.

Then nothing.

I ran to the surface. The ash had stopped falling. The sky was clear, for the first time in decades. Stars. Actual stars, not hidden behind clouds of poison.

"Yumi," I whispered.

The communicator in my hand buzzed. A message. Text only:

"I'm sorry, Jax. I had to. Be safe. Be boring. Be human(ish). --Y"

I read it until the screen went dark.

Then I sat in the snow, under the stars, and I cried.


CHAPTER 16
Sebastian Blackwood was not a man who accepted failure.

He sat in his citadel, surrounded by cakes he didn't eat and screens he didn't watch, and he felt the absence of the Catalyst like a missing tooth.

"She destroyed it," he said to no one. "The girl destroyed the Catalyst."

His aide nodded, trembling.

"And Vyx?"

"Weakened, sir. Not destroyed. But... diminished."

Blackwood stood. He walked to the window, looked down at the planet below. It was healing. The ash was clearing. The poison was fading.

"She chose death over power," he murmured. "How... quaint."

He turned. "Prepare the next phase. If we can't control the Catalyst, we'll build our own. Something better. Something that doesn't offer choice."

"Sir? What abo-"

"Project OMEGA. Begin immediately."

The aide bowed and scurried away.

Blackwood returned to the window. He smiled.

"The game isn't over, little mouse. It's just beginning."

EPILOGUE
In the void, Vyx watched.

She was diminished, yes. Weakened by the girl's sacrifice, yes. But gone, no. Never gone.

"Interesting," she whispered to the darkness. "She chose death. The coward's granddaughter chose life. And the little mouse..."

She smiled.

"The little mouse chooses to fight."

She turned her gaze to Mars, to the facilities where Blackwood schemed, to the depths of space where new Catalysts waited to be born.

"Let them fight," she said. "Let them build. Let them hope."

Her eyes glowed with ancient light.

"Hope is the sweetest bait. And I am very, very patient."
